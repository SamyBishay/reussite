# Rule 8: Decisions get a method of their own

**The rule.** A decision is not a card. Define the problem, list every option, weigh the pros and cons of doing and of not doing each one from 1 to 5, choose, turn the choice into cards, and review it on a date.

**Grade: practice only** for the weighted pros and cons sheet. **Some support** for the problem-solving steps around it, which have been tested, mostly for depression. Research on pros and cons also gives a caution.

## What the studies found

- A meta-analysis of problem-solving therapy for depression (21 samples) found it as effective as other talking treatments and medication, and better than no treatment. It worked better when it trained all four skills (define the problem, generate options, decide, carry out and check) and a positive attitude to problems. (Bell and D'Zurilla, 2009.)
- An updated meta-analysis of 30 trials (3,530 people) found an effect against control groups of g = 0.79, with very uneven results. The nine trials with the lowest risk of bias gave g = 0.34, comparable to other therapies. So the honest size is small to moderate. (Cuijpers and colleagues, 2018.)
- A review of motivational interviewing found that with people who are torn about changing, a pros and cons exercise tends to lower their commitment to change, while drawing out their own reasons promotes change. Once a person has decided, a pros and cons exercise may strengthen commitment. (Miller and Rose, 2015.)
- In a small study of 47 men with no control group, listing pros and cons of drinking less was followed, a month later, by more motivation and less drinking. Without a control group, the effect of the exercise cannot be separated from the effect of being asked about drinking. (LaBrie and colleagues, 2006.)
- Across 12 behaviours, how people rate pros and cons follows a regular pattern with the stage of change. This describes people. It does not test whether doing the exercise helps. (Prochaska and colleagues, 1994.)

## What they do not show

- No study we found tests Réussite's form: four columns, each item weighted from 1 to 5 and added up. Weighted scoring of pros and cons has, as far as we found, no direct test, in ADHD or in anyone.
- The problem-solving evidence is for depression, not ADHD, and for courses led by a therapist.
- Writing pros and cons has mixed results: it can help after a person has decided and can weaken commitment while a person is still torn. That is a caution, not proof of harm.
- Nothing we opened tests reviewing a decision on a set date.
- The decision sheet is usually traced to a 1977 book by Janis and Mann. We did not read it.
