# The method as a whole, and the weekly review

**What this covers.** The nine rules together, which come from CBT skills programmes for adults with ADHD, and the weekly review.

**Grade: some support** for CBT skills programmes. **Practice only** for the weekly review, and for the app on its own.

## What the studies found

- In a randomised trial of 86 adults with ADHD still symptomatic on medication, 12 sessions of CBT beat relaxation with educational support on blinded assessor ratings, and the gain was kept at 12 months. (Safren and colleagues, 2010.)
- In a randomised trial of 88 adults, a 12-week group programme teaching time management, organisation and planning beat supportive therapy. It is the programme closest to what Réussite teaches. (Solanto and colleagues, 2010.)
- A meta-analysis of nine trials found CBT better than a waiting list (SMD 0.76, moderate to large; 4 trials, 160 people) and better, by less, than active comparison treatments (SMD 0.43, small to moderate; 3 trials, 191 people). (Young and colleagues, 2020.)
- A meta-analysis of 28 studies found CBT reduced core ADHD symptoms and also depression and anxiety, and raised self-esteem and quality of life. The authors call their support "cautiously optimistic". (Liu and colleagues, 2023.)
- Three trials of internet-based or app-based programmes, all self-rated: a self-guided course (120 adults) gave moderate-to-large gains over an online information module, and only 29 per cent finished every module (Kenter and colleagues, 2023); internet CBT (104 adults) helped more than usual care, but an internet relaxation programme did about as well (Nasri and colleagues, 2023); a fully self-guided app added to usual care (337 adults) gave lower self-rated symptoms at 3 months (D'Amelio and colleagues, 2026).
- A meta-analysis of correlations found that people who plan and prioritise their time do moderately better at work and school and report better wellbeing. These are correlations in general working and student groups. (Aeon, Faber and Panaccio, 2021.) Planning ahead when and where to act also has support: see [rule 9](research-9.md).

## What they do not show

- Most trials are small, run in specialist clinics, with a therapist. Almost none test planning skills on their own: they test a whole package of planning, coping with distraction and coping with unhelpful thoughts.
- Much of the benefit is on self-rated symptoms. The two 2010 trials also have blinded raters, but effects on everyday outcomes such as work, money and tasks done are measured much less often.
- Against a relaxation or support group the effect shrinks. Part of the benefit is attention and structure, not the specific skills.
- No result we opened shows that a planning app by itself, without a clinician, helps adults with ADHD. The self-guided trials test structured courses, not a daily planner, and many people drop out.
- No trial tests a weekly review or a daily planning session on its own, in ADHD or otherwise. Whether planning causes better results, or organised people simply plan more, the correlations cannot say.
- We did not open the CBT manuals, a 2025 review of CBT for adult ADHD (no abstract was available), or the NICE guideline on ADHD (its page would not load), so we say nothing of what they hold.
