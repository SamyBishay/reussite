# Rule 2: Judge urgency and importance separately

**The rule.** Urgent and important are two questions, and they give four boxes: Now, Protect, Quick and Later. The important card with no clock, Protect, gets a slot every day, or it never happens.

**Grade: practice only** for the four boxes and the daily slot: we found no test of either. The reasons behind them have some support. Urgency pulls on choices by itself, and in ADHD the pull of the sooner reward is stronger.

## What the studies found

- In five experiments, people often chose an unimportant task with a lower payoff over an important one when the unimportant one only looked urgent, for example because it seemed about to expire. Urgency pulled by itself, apart from real consequences. (Zhu, Yang and Hsee, 2018.)
- A meta-analysis of 21 studies (3,913 people) found that delay discounting, preferring a smaller sooner reward to a larger later one, is higher in ADHD, with a medium effect (d = 0.43). Real or pretend rewards, and age, made no significant difference. (Jackson and MacKillop, 2016.)
- A second meta-analysis, 37 comparisons (3,763 people), found that people with ADHD choose small immediate rewards over larger delayed ones more often than other people, with small-to-medium effects. (Marx and colleagues, 2021.)
- In a randomised trial of 88 adults with ADHD, a 12-week group programme that taught time management, organisation and planning beat supportive therapy on inattention, with about five times the odds of responding (odds ratio 5.41). The abstract does not mention prioritising. (Solanto and colleagues, 2010.)

## What they do not show

- No trial we found tests a four-box urgent and important grid, or giving the important card with no clock a slot every day. That is a negative result from a limited search (journal abstracts and DOI records), not proof that none exists.
- The first study is not about ADHD or lists. It supports keeping the two ideas apart only indirectly.
- The choice studies are lab tasks, mostly with money. They do not show that deadlines or planning rules fix choices in real life. The second one mixes children and adults.
- Rating schemes such as A, B and C in CBT manuals: we did not read the manuals.
