# Rule 1: Write everything down

**The rule.** Everything you have to do is a card, in the app: not in your head, not in a chat.

**Grade: some support.** A plan for an unfinished task quiets nagging thoughts about it, and CBT programmes for adult ADHD work. Nobody has isolated the writing down in adults with ADHD.

## What the studies found

- In a series of lab experiments, unfinished goals came back as intrusive thoughts, made goal words easier to notice and made people do worse on an unrelated task. When people made a specific plan for the goal, these effects went away. (Masicampo and Baumeister, 2011.)
- A review describes "cognitive offloading": using an action or a tool, such as a reminder or a note, so that the mind has less to hold. It is common and has only recently been studied in its own right. The review offers a framework for when people do it. (Risko and Gilbert, 2016.)
- In a randomised trial of 86 adults with ADHD who still had symptoms on medication, 12 sessions of CBT gave lower symptom ratings from a blinded assessor than relaxation with educational support, and the gain was still there at 12 months. (Safren and colleagues, 2010.)
- A meta-analysis of 32 studies (up to 896 adults with ADHD) found that CBT programmes beat control conditions, with small-to-medium effects on self-reported symptoms and on functioning. Results varied, and the effects were smaller against active controls. (Knouse and colleagues, 2017.)

## What they do not show

- The lab experiments are about goals and plans in general, in people who were not selected for ADHD. They show that a plan quiets the mind. They do not show that a written list does, or that it helps adults with ADHD.
- The review does not test lists or ADHD.
- The two CBT sources test whole programmes. They cannot say which part did the work, and their abstracts do not show a "one place for everything" rule.
- We did not read the CBT manuals these programmes use.
