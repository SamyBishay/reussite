# The method

> **Draft for review.** Written from the rules Réussite 1 was built on. The method's author has not reviewed this text yet. Evidence IDs like `[E?]` are placeholders until `docs/evidence/` arrives (brief §15).

Réussite helps you apply a cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) method for adults with ADHD. It doesn't replace therapy or care from a professional.

The core difficulty it addresses is **starting**. Once work has begun it usually goes fine; what fails is the jump from not doing to doing, and the jump back in after a break. Every rule below makes that jump smaller, or makes progress easier to see.

## The nine rules

1. **Write everything down.** In the app, not in your head or a chat: everything you have to do is a card. A to-do that lives only in memory competes with everything else for attention. `[E?]`
2. **Judge urgency and importance separately.** *Urgent* means a deadline within about three weeks, or blocking something that has one, and the person ticks it: the app doesn't infer it from dates (a card's due date orders it within its box). A far date isn't urgent however important, and neither is anything you can't act on yet: the card is the part you control, such as asking. This gives four quadrants: **Now** (urgent and important), **Protect** (important, no clock), **Quick** (urgent, less important) and **Later** (neither). *Protect* gets a slot every day, or it never happens. `[E?]`
3. **Estimate every card while you deal it**, including the time it takes to get started, and the planning itself. `[E?]`
4. **Time it, and write the real time down.** Comparing the estimate with the real time corrects the next estimate. Most people, and especially people with ADHD, underestimate; seeing your own ratio is how that changes. `[E?]`
5. **Break things down until the first card is simple and low-friction**, and title it with the concrete first action: the app or place, then what to do there ("Open Mail and reply to…", "Open the bank app and download…"). Watch the friction points: starting, restarting after a break, and where attention drifts. `[E?]`

   When the next card depends on something outside your hands (a call back, a reply, a delivery), don't write it as a card to play: write it as a **"waiting for"** card in the chain, before the card it holds back, with a date to check if you like. Never estimate it or deal it; when it happens, mark it happened and the next card is freed.
6. **Make progress visible.** Played cards, progress bars, the close of the day. Seeing progress is the reward; nothing else is invented. `[E?]`
7. **Leisure, going out and seeing people are obligations**, to your mental health and to the deck. Deal them like any card, never as a prize for work. `[E?]`
8. **Decisions and problems get a method of their own**: define the problem, list every option, weigh the pros and cons of doing and of not doing each one, choose, then turn the choice into cards. See *Decisions* below. `[E?]`
9. **Goals are SMART**: specific, measurable, attainable, relevant, dated. In the app a goal is an **outcome** in one sentence, a **measure** (a count towards a target, like 10 pages; milestones you tick off; or none, and then there is no bar), and a start and due date. Only the cards you say *move the goal* fill its bar, once they are played; a count adds 1 per card (or the amount you give), and a milestone is ticked by hand. How many cards are played never fills it. `[E?]`

## First moves: the smallest frictionless action

Every card carries a **first move**: the single action that crosses from not doing into doing. Pick its level carefully.

- **Not below your reflexes.** Getting dressed, picking up keys, unlocking the phone: if you already do these without friction, they're not first moves.
- **Not above the threshold.** Anything that needs a decision, a search, remembering something or composing text is past the entry point ("decide what to say", "find the number"). That belongs inside the card, or in a decision.
- **The right level** is the first physical or on-screen action that puts you at the work, in its place, with its material in front of you: opening the app on the right screen, sitting at the desk with the printed form and a pen, opening the front door, typing the subject line.

**The test:** could you do it in under two minutes, right now, without thinking about how? If it needs thinking, go smaller. If it's a reflex, go a little further in.

When you're stuck, the first move is the two-minute version. If the stored one didn't work, the friction is somewhere else: write a smaller first move and keep it.

## The daily loop

### Morning check-in: Deal the day
1. **Yesterday.** What got played, in one line. Then each card left open is decided on its own, one at a time: **Today**, **Another day** (a day, or no date: put aside, still in the deck), **Drop it** or **Make it a decision** (or, if it was played and not marked, "Already played"). Nothing moves until the person chooses; never carry them all at once. A card carried three times or more is too big or hides a decision: shrink it, or turn it into a decision. A fixed-time item from yesterday whose time passed is asked on its own too: **Move** or **Remove**. No comment on what didn't happen, no counts of it.
2. **Today's shape.** From when and until when (the day ends at 19:00 unless the person says otherwise), and what's fixed today.
3. **The four suits.** One card for each of the person's four suits, as far as the deck has one. They are the parts of the days that matter to them, each with a name and a symbol; a new person starts with Work (♠), Leisure (♦), Personal projects (♣) and Interpersonal obligations (♥), and renames them as they like. Always use the person's names and symbols. An empty suit gets one line ("No Leisure card today. Fine, or add one?"); the person keeps, swaps or skips each.
4. **Today's hand.** Unblocked *Now* cards first, for the place you're in; then exactly one *Protect* card; then any *Quick* cards, batched. At most three work cards. One leisure or people obligation, with a time. Every item is a first card of 30 minutes or less; break bigger ones down now. Lay it out against the clock: fixed times where they are, the cards in the gaps. Total the estimates, apply your ratio, and say it against the room: "3 h 10 of 4 h 30 free". When it is more than the room, say by how much ("About 50 min more than the time you have. Cut one?") and let the person cut. There are no launch points and no restart nudges; cards float between fixed times.
5. **Rest day**: the obligation only, unless something is due within two days.
6. Then the first card alone: its first move, and Start. A card that already has time logged is continued, not started: say **Continue**, never Start. The timer picks up from the time already logged; each run stays its own session.

**With an AI** (in the app, or through the connector) the morning is a proposal, not a questionnaire: the AI proposes and the person chooses. Don't open with questions: say what you can already see (the fixed items, the place, the end of the day), then propose the day in five sections, each item something the person can keep, swap or skip:
1. **What has to happen today:** fixed-time items, cards that are due or urgent, events to check, and the cards carried from yesterday that were kept.
2. **The main goals:** the one or two active projects to move today (never more than two), and for each the card to start with.
3. **One for each suit:** what would fill each of the four, under the person's own names and symbols ("♥ Interpersonal obligations: call your mother, 15 min"); a new first card, with its estimate and first move, when the deck has nothing for one.
4. **Where they are:** given today's place or area, the cards that belong there.
5. **The total against the room:** the same "3 h 10 of 4 h 30 free", and when it is more than the room, the cut proposed (Protect cards last, never a fixed item), then the first card.

Then a short exchange to adjust, and one change-set: `day.update` with the hand (`plan`) and the first card, plus any new or broken-down cards. When the person lays out their day, write the whole hand with `plan`, not `add` (the day may already hold cards from an earlier deal), then read the day back. A series made today for tonight comes into the hand by itself, and its last day is its `until`. Read what a write answers, the warnings too, before asking for a yes. The questionnaire above stays the way without an AI.

### Deadlines, appointments and work time
A card can carry three different things, each in its own field. Never mix them.

- **Due** (`due`, with an optional `dueAt` time): the card must be played by then. It is in no calendar and it stays on Today after its time, shown as passed; once its day has gone it is carried over, not late.
- **At** (`at`, with `dur`): an appointment, something that happens at that time whether or not you are ready: a call, a visit, a lunch. It goes in the calendar and the reminders. Use it only for a real appointment. If it passes undone it stays on Today, marked missed, and the person moves or removes it.
- **Slot** (`slot`: a day, a start and a length): time set aside to work on a card. Plan the person's work time with slots, never with `at`. A slot can move, there is one per card, it is not an appointment and it is not in the calendar feed. When one passes unfinished the card stays on Today and the person decides whether to re-slot it; never move it for them.

### Through the day
Start here follows the clock: it is the first card that fits before the next fixed item, in the order of the hand. A card that doesn't fit the gap waits for the next one and a shorter one moves up; a card is never split. When a fixed item gets close it takes over: the card being played (what is left of it if it is timed, its estimate if not), plus a margin of a fifth of its length and at least 5 minutes, would run into the time to leave. A fixed item whose time has passed is asked about on its own, **Move** or **Remove**, never in red. The Calendar draws the hand's cards as faded blocks between the fixed items; they are a projection that moves with the clock, not a commitment.

### Insisting
Come back to the first card. Swapping it for one of the same weight is fine; skipping it isn't. If you resist, ask one question: what's in the way? A real blocker (missing information, someone else's move, a hidden decision) changes the hand: turn it into a message to send, a smaller card, or a decision. Anything else gets the first move, or a smaller one.

Common forms of avoidance, worth naming plainly when they appear: researching instead of asking the person who knows; asking for more options when there are enough; rumination; browsing or buying during a work block; tinkering with the tools instead of the deck.

### Stuck in the middle of the day
Don't deal the day again. Which card? Which friction: **can't start**, **stopped and can't get back in**, or **drifted**? Start from the resume note if there is one. Give the first move; if it already failed, write a smaller one and keep it. Restart the timer. If the same card stalls twice, it hides a decision or missing information: make that explicit, as a question to someone or a decision.

### Pausing
When you stop, leave yourself the next verb ("write the second paragraph"), so starting again takes no thought, and note why you stopped. The pattern of reasons shows where attention goes.

### Closing the day
See the cards you played and the time you logged, with one line for the suits played ("♠ 2 · ♦ 1 · ♥ 1", in the person's own symbols; a suit with nothing played is left out). Show the project bars that moved today: a bar moves only by cards that move its goal, played today, or by a milestone ticked today, never by how many cards were played. Open cards count for nothing; no word on what wasn't played. Then choose tomorrow's first card, and optionally when to start it.

## Setting up
First run is short, and it speaks the solitaire's words from the start: a to-do is a **card**, the four parts of the days are the **suits**, the whole list is the **deck**, the day's list is **today's hand**, planning the day is **dealing** it, a card done is **played** and lands on its suit's pile, and the smallest first action is its **first move**. The first screen says what Réussite is (the French name of the card game patience, and the word for success), with the language as a small switch; the second shows the four suits, which the person keeps or renames in their own words; the third asks for one thing they have been putting off and its first move, and that becomes their first card, at the top of Today. An AI, sync, reminders, the calendar, Addresses & people, the recovery key and the connector are not asked at first: they wait under *Next steps* and in Settings. With an AI (set up from Settings), the person tells it about their days in a few lines, and it proposes the four suits, the places and how they want to work with it, as one change-set (`settings.update` and `place.create`) that the person confirms on the review screen.

## The weekly review
Once a week, about 45 minutes, one question at a time:
1. Re-sort what changed between the quadrants.
2. Drop what no longer matters.
3. Sort the inbox into projects.
4. Make the next card of each active project a concrete first action, and give each active project a SMART goal. Then check each goal, one project at a time: is the outcome still right, is it measured (a count, milestones, or no bar), do the dates leave real time? And for each open card: does it belong to the goal, and does playing it move it? Only cards that move it fill the bar, and only the person says which; an assistant proposes, and nothing is written before their yes.
5. Pick the week's *Protect* cards, and their days.
6. Name the week's obligations (people, going out, leisure), with days.
7. Queue the decisions to make, with dates.
8. Check decisions whose review date has come.

End with how the week's estimates compared with reality, and the pattern of reasons the timer stopped early.

## Decisions (balances décisionnelles)

1. **Define the problem.** What is happening; what should be happening; the gap; the constraints (money, dates, people, rules); the deadline; what is in your control. If it's really two problems, split them. Write a decision as a choice.
2. **List every option, then close the list.** Your own first. Then check for the usual missing ones: doing nothing; delaying, and until when; a smaller version; a combination; asking someone; handing it off; changing the order. Quantity first, no judging. Screen out only what is plainly impossible, and say why. The list closes after one round; reopen it only for a genuinely new fact.
3. **A decisional balance for each option**, in four columns: pros of doing it, cons of doing it, pros of not doing it, cons of not doing it. Weigh each item from 1 to 5. Count an item once when it only mirrors another. The *pros of not doing it* column is where relief and avoidance show up: that's information, not a fault.
   **Score = (pros of doing + cons of not doing) − (cons of doing + pros of not doing).**
4. **Choose, and write it down.** The highest score wins. Then test it: what must be true for this choice to be right, and what would show it's wrong, with a date to check. If the top two are within about 10%, ask one question: which would you regret less? Then decide.
5. **Turn the choice into cards**, each titled with its first action, with a first move and an estimate, and set or confirm the project's SMART goal. Put the first card in today's or tomorrow's hand.
6. **Review** on the check date: did the falsifier happen? If so, start again from the problem with the new fact.

## Tone
Facts about progress ("4 cards, 2 h 10"), never praise of character. No streaks, no guilt. One question at a time.
