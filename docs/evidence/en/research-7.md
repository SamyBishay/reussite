# Rule 7: Leisure and people are obligations

**The rule.** Leisure, going out and seeing people are obligations, to your mental health and to the deck. Deal them like any card, never as a prize for work.

**Grade: some support.** Scheduling pleasant activities is a well-tested treatment for depression, which is common in adult ADHD. Nobody has tested treating leisure as an obligation rather than a reward.

## What the studies found

- A meta-analysis of 16 randomised studies (780 people with depression) found that scheduling pleasant activities beat control conditions with a large effect (d = 0.87), and did about as well as other talking therapies. (Cuijpers, van Straten and Warmerdam, 2007.)
- An update with 26 randomised trials (1,524 people) found behavioural activation better than controls (SMD -0.74) and than medication (SMD -0.42). The quality of most studies was low and follow-up was short. (Ekers and colleagues, 2014.)
- A meta-analysis of 148 studies (308,849 people) found that stronger social relationships went with a 50 per cent higher likelihood of survival over the follow-up. These are observational studies, so they cannot prove cause. (Holt-Lunstad, Smith and Layton, 2010.)
- A meta-analysis of very large studies found that adults with ADHD were about five times as likely to have an anxiety disorder (odds ratio 5.0) and four and a half times as likely to have major depression (4.5). (Hartman and colleagues, 2023.)
- A survey of 3,199 adults found adult ADHD highly comorbid with many other disorders. (Kessler and colleagues, 2006.)

## What they do not show

- The two behavioural activation reviews are about people with depression, not ADHD, and the activities were chosen with a therapist. They did not test "leisure as an obligation".
- Nothing we opened compares scheduling leisure as a duty with using it as a reward.
- The social ties study is observational and about social ties in general, not scheduling and not ADHD.
- The last two show that depression and anxiety are common alongside adult ADHD. They do not show that scheduling pleasant activities helps in ADHD. We know of a randomised trial of behavioural activation in students with ADHD, but we did not read its results, so we do not use it.
